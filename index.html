Learning report

From a repeated noun to a translation pattern

Contents

Origin Background Gap Questions Method Analysis Conclusion Reflection

Origin

I began with a simple observation from the supplied NV Atlas sample: English subtitle lines often turn an action into a noun, while the Chinese line lets the action remain a verb. The corpus makes that small grammatical shift visible at scale.

Background

Nominalization is common in English, especially in formal or compressed dialogue. Chinese can preserve a nominal frame, but it can also recast the event as a direct verb or use a light-verb construction. These choices affect rhythm, emphasis, and how directly an action is presented.

Gap

Existing subtitle corpora are large, but they rarely make this particular contrast easy to inspect. A focused explorer can connect a sentence pair, its key term, and its translation strategy in one view, while keeping the original source traceable.

Questions

How often is an English nominalization rendered as a direct Chinese verb? Which terms show the widest range of renderings? Does the pattern hold across the most frequent terms, and what does that suggest about subtitle translation norms?

Method

The corpus contains 500 English-Simplified Chinese sentence pairs from OPUS OpenSubtitles 2024. The supplied CSV records each pair's 1-based sentence ID; I verified those IDs against the official Moses-format release. I retained the supplied key terms and added source, category, human_checked, unit, and rationale fields. Categories were applied with a fixed, documented rule set. A random sample of 50 entries (seed 2026) is listed in the data metadata for manual verification; entries are marked human_checked only after that review.

The sample is not a random sample of the full OpenSubtitles collection. It is a focused set of lines containing English nominalizations, so the category proportions describe this corpus, not all subtitles.

Analysis

The network shows that a small group of high-frequency terms accounts for much of the data. Attention, permission, vacation, protection, and performance are especially prominent. The treemap and heatmap make the strategy mix visible: direct verb recasting is the largest category, followed by retained nominal frames and a smaller set of light-verb constructions.

The pattern is not a simple one-to-one mapping. The same English term can be rendered with different Chinese verbs, and the selected term view exposes those alternatives. This supports a norm-oriented reading: subtitle translators tend to favor compact, action-oriented Chinese, but they retain nominal framing when the line needs a more formal or event-like emphasis.

Conclusion

The explorer turns a repeated grammatical habit into a searchable translation pattern. It shows that nominalization is not merely a word-level issue: it is a choice about how an event is packaged for the viewer.

Reflection

The hardest part was separating a defensible category rule from a plausible-looking label. I learned to check the source IDs, keep the sampling method visible, and treat the charts as evidence to verify against the table rather than decoration. AI helped structure the site and draft the first pass, but the category boundaries and source checks still need human judgment.

Codebook

Three ways of handling an English nominalization

The rules are applied in order. The first matching category wins.

01

Verb recast

The English nominalization is expressed as a direct Chinese verb, with no light-verb frame or explicit nominal marker.

Rule: no light-verb marker and no nominal marker in the Chinese line.

“What is his obsession with you?” → “他咋对你这么痴迷?”

02

Nominal retained

The Chinese line keeps an event-like or nominal frame, often with 的, 性, 化, 度, 感, or a similar marker.

Rule: no light-verb marker, but at least one nominal marker is present.

“A new and flaming engine of destruction...” → “...尝试烧毁...”

03

Light verb frame

The action is carried by a light verb or verbal frame such as 进行, 加以, 予以, 作出, or 受到.

Rule: at least one light-verb marker is present; this takes priority over nominal markers.

“...authorized to order punishment.” → “...可以下令进行处罚”

Codebook log

The first pass used a broad list of nominal markers. It over-counted lines where 的 was only a possessive or relative marker. I kept the marker rule for transparency, but added the light-verb category first so explicit verbal frames are not mislabeled as retained nominals. The category counts should be read as a reproducible first-pass annotation, not a definitive linguistic analysis.

AI use declaration

What I used AI for, and what I checked

Tools

OpenAI Codex (GPT-5-based coding agent) for data preparation, interface implementation, and drafting. D3.js for the visualizations.

AI did

It helped normalize the CSV, draft the codebook rules, build the static site, and write the first version of the report.

I checked

I verified the source IDs against the official corpus and reviewed the category rules. The 50-entry verification sample is queued for manual checking; entries are not marked human_checked until that review is complete.

One AI mistake

The first category pass treated every 的 as evidence of a retained nominal, which misread ordinary possessive phrases. I noticed it by comparing the rule against sample lines and revised the rule order.
